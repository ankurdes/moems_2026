🧩 Team brain teaser (10 min)

Work on it together as a team.

1The pizza cutter
Answer: 16
  1. Try small cases. 0 cuts: 1 piece. 1 cut: 2 pieces. 2 cuts that cross: 4 pieces. 3 cuts: 7 pieces.
  2. Why 7 and not 8? A new cut adds one piece for every region it passes through. A line can cross each earlier line at most once, so the 3rd cut crosses 2 lines and passes through at most 3 regions, adding 3 pieces.
  3. So the nth cut adds at most n pieces: 1, 2, 4, 7, 11, 16.
  4. With 5 cuts: 1 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 16. You get it when every pair of cuts crosses inside the pizza and no three cuts meet at one point.
⚡ Trick: When you can't picture the big case, list the small cases, find out how much each step adds, and explain why. The pattern 1, 2, 4, 7, 11, 16 comes from adding 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.

1Exponents
Answer: 128
  1. Four copies of the same thing: 212 + 212 + 212 + 212 = 4 × 212.
  2. 4 = 22, so this is 22 × 212 = 214.
  3. √(214) = 27 = 128.
⚡ Trick: Adding equal powers is multiplying: n copies of 2k is n × 2k. Turn everything into powers of 2, then a square root just halves the exponent.
2Digits
Answer: 358
  1. Prime factor: 120 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 5.
  2. Fewer digits means a smaller number. Two digits can make at most 9 × 9 = 81, so we need at least 3 digits.
  3. Group the primes into 3 digits (each 9 or less): 5 must be its own digit. 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 splits into 8 × 3 or 4 × 6 (or 2 × 12, which fails).
  4. That gives digits {3, 5, 8} or {4, 5, 6}. Put the smallest digit first: 358 and 456.
  5. The smallest is 358.
⚡ Trick: To make the smallest number: first use as few digits as possible, then put the digits in increasing order.
3Counting digits
Answer: 131
  1. From 1 to 99, the digit 3 shows up 10 times in the ones place (3, 13, ..., 93) and 10 times in the tens place (30 to 39). That's 20.
  2. From 100 on, count each 3: 103 (21), 113 (22), 123 (23), 130 (24), 131 (25).
  3. Locker 132 would make it 26. So the last locker is 131.
⚡ Trick: Count digits by place value: how many times in the ones place, then in the tens place. Then walk forward carefully, counting a number like 133 twice.
4Algebra
Answer: 84
  1. Call the sides a and b. Three sides means two of one and one of the other.
  2. Ann: 2a + b = 26. Ben: a + 2b = 31.
  3. Add them: 3a + 3b = 57, so a + b = 19.
  4. Subtract from Ann's: a = 26 − 19 = 7. Then b = 12.
  5. Area = 7 × 12 = 84.
⚡ Trick: Two equations that look alike? Add them first. That often gives a simple total that makes the rest easy.
5Geometry
Answer: 150
  1. The angles of a pentagon add up to (5 − 2) × 180 = 540°.
  2. The ratio has 3 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 18 parts, so each part is 540 ÷ 18 = 30°.
  3. The largest angle is 5 parts: 5 × 30 = 150°.
⚡ Trick: Angle sum of an n-sided polygon: (n − 2) × 180°. For ratios, add the parts, then find one part.
6Probability
Answer: 19/66
  1. Ordered draws: 12 choices for the first marble, then 11 for the second, so 132 equally likely ways.
  2. Both red: 3 × 2 = 6. Both blue: 4 × 3 = 12. Both green: 5 × 4 = 20.
  3. Same color: 6 + 12 + 20 = 38 ways.
  4. Probability: 38/132 = 19/66.
⚡ Trick: Without replacement, the second draw has one fewer marble overall and one fewer of the color you took. Count each color case and add.
7Counting
Answer: 60
  1. Slide the rectangle around. A rectangle 2 wide can start in 7 − 2 + 1 = 6 columns. A rectangle 3 tall can start in 7 − 3 + 1 = 5 rows.
  2. 2 wide × 3 tall: 6 × 5 = 30 places.
  3. 3 wide × 2 tall: 5 × 6 = 30 places.
  4. Total: 60.
⚡ Trick: Placing an a × b shape on an n × n board: (n − a + 1) × (n − b + 1). Don't forget to turn it the other way.
8Counting
Answer: 84
  1. No digit can be 0: it would have to come first, and a 3-digit number can't start with 0.
  2. Pick any 3 different digits from 1 to 9. There is exactly one way to put them in increasing order. For example, {7, 1, 4} gives only 147.
  3. So the answer is the number of ways to choose 3 digits from 9: (9 × 8 × 7) ÷ (3 × 2 × 1) = 84.
⚡ Trick: When the order is forced (increasing, decreasing, alphabetical), you're really just choosing which items to use, not arranging them.
9Number theory (hard)
Answer: 13
  1. From 10! on, every factorial contains 2 × 5 twice (from 2, 5, 10 and more), so it ends in at least 00. Those terms don't change the last two digits.
  2. So only 1! to 9! matter. Keep just the last two digits of each:
    1! = 1, 2! = 2, 3! = 6, 4! = 24, 5! = 120 → 20, 6! = 720 → 20, 7! = 5040 → 40, 8! = 40320 → 20, 9! = 362880 → 80.
  3. Add: 1 + 2 + 6 + 24 + 20 + 20 + 40 + 20 + 80 = 213.
  4. The last two digits are 13.
⚡ Trick: For "last digits" of a huge sum, find when the terms start ending in zeros and ignore them. Then only keep the last two digits as you go.
10Geometry (hard)
Answer: 50
  1. Upright squares. Size 1: 4 × 4 = 16. Size 2: 3 × 3 = 9. Size 3: 2 × 2 = 4. Size 4: 1. Total 30.
  2. The trick: every tilted square fits snugly inside an upright "frame" square, with its corners on the frame's sides. A frame of size k holds k squares: the frame itself plus k − 1 tilted ones.
  3. So count frames and multiply: size 1: 16 × 1 = 16. Size 2: 9 × 2 = 18. Size 3: 4 × 3 = 12. Size 4: 1 × 4 = 4.
  4. Total: 16 + 18 + 12 + 4 = 50. (A computer check of every set of 4 dots agrees.)
⚡ Trick: Tilted squares are hard to count directly. Instead, count the upright square that each one sits inside, and multiply by how many squares fit in it.
